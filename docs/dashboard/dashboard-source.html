The research question

Can a six-legged robot feel slippery or soft ground before it steps on it?

The robot is a small hexapod (Interbotix PhantomX Mark IV, 18 hobby servos). The proposed method, called Visual–Proprioceptive Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture VP-JEPA, looks at the ground ahead with a depth-and-colour camera and predicts what the legs will feel 60, 200 and 400 milliseconds in the future, then walks using that prediction. The front legs see their footholds in the camera; what the front legs feel is then passed back to the middle and rear legs, which cannot see their own footholds. The claim is only worth making if it beats carefully matched alternatives, so most of the work so far builds those alternatives (the "comparison baselines") under one shared training recipe.

What has been built

  • A realistic simulation of the robot: servo model with dropped sensor readings, sensor noise, camera-mast weight, random payload.
  • A walking policy trained from scratch that follows speed and turning commands, stands still on command and crosses generated terrain (rough ground, slopes, bumps, stairs).
  • Ground made of ten different materials that change from place to place (Teflon, talc, wet tile, foam, rubber, concrete and others).
  • The blind "trunk" every comparison arm will start from: a body-sensing encoder, a velocity and foot-force estimator, and a set of physics quiz questions that force the encoder to learn about the ground.

What comes next

  • Write up the memory-window sweep (25 vs 50 vs 75 steps) and fix the window for every arm.
  • Finish and evaluate the blind-trunk seeds (seeds 43–45 done, 46 running).
  • Measure how many camera-equipped simulations the GPU can run at once; this currently blocks the camera stages.
  • Run the headroom test: does knowing the ground material ahead help at all (Oracle vs Blind)? Nothing with a camera starts before this passes.

    Robot & simulator

    The robot, its servos and the simulated world

    Everything is trained in NVIDIA Isaac Sim / Isaac Lab (a physics simulator using the PhysX engine) and must later run on the real robot, so the simulator deliberately includes the robot's imperfections: cheap servos with limited torque, lost sensor readings, noise and delays.

    Robot facts

    Servo model AX-12A

    Each joint is a Dynamixel AX-12A hobby servo. The simulator reproduces how it really behaves instead of an ideal motor:

    • Position-only control with a soft spring: the servo pushes toward its target with a gentle "compliance" slope (32 ticks), a small dead zone (1 tick) and a minimum push ("punch").
    • Torque limit 1.5 N·m that falls off as the joint speeds up (no-load speed 59 revolutions per minute ≈ 6.18 rad/s).
    • Coarse position sensor: 10-bit, 0.29° per tick.
    • Gear play (backlash) 0.5°, bus delay 5–10 ms, rotor inertia 0.003 kg·m².
    • Dropped readings: each servo read can fail, more often under load (probability = 0.005 + 0.068 × torque ÷ stall torque, about 5–7 %). A failed read repeats the last good value, and an 18-number "fresh reading" mask tells the policy which values are stale.
    • Joint speed is never read from the simulator's perfect velocity: it is computed the way the real robot must, from two position readings 40 ms apart.

    Values are datasheet estimates Measure later — a bench measurement of gain, backlash, latency and drop rate is still needed.

    Why the resting pose changed

    The original (Phase 1) resting pose needed 134 % of the servo's stall torque in the femur joint when three legs carried the robot — physically impossible for the real servos. Measured in the simulator from contact force × lever arm (the simulator's own torque readout under-reads by about 2×):

    Generated terrain

    A curriculum of 15 difficulty levels (0–14) is used for training; a robot moves up a level when it covers at least 80 % of its commanded path, and down if it falls or covers less than 40 %. Levels 15–20 are never trained on and are kept as a held-out test.

    Ground materials (blind trunk onward)

    The ground is divided into 4 m cells of irregular patches (one material, two materials split by a line, stripes or a mosaic), with blended edges up to 0.8 m wide. The layout is fixed so every arm and seed walks the same map; each episode redraws the exact values inside each material's range. "Shiny" marks materials that will look glossy to the camera.     Randomised every episode

      Commands the robot must follow

      • Forward speed 0.05–0.20 m/s, sideways ±0.05 m/s, turning ±0.4 rad/s; a new command every 5 seconds.
      • 10 % of segments: stand still. Of the rest: 15 % turn on the spot, 30 % walk while drifting or turning, 55 % walk straight.
      • Pass/fail evaluation uses 0.10 and 0.15 m/s straight ahead.
      • Episodes last 10 s (500 control steps). An episode ends early if the body sinks to within 5 cm of the ground below it.

      Two loops running at different speeds

      Control loop — 50 times per second (every 20 ms) Built

      Read the 18 servo positions (about 4–5 ms on the servo bus) → body-sensing encoder → velocity estimator → policy → write 18 joint targets (about 0.6 ms). On the robot this runs on the CPU of the Jetson Orin Nano computer and must finish within 1.5 ms of computing. In the simulator, physics runs 4 times faster (200 per second).

      Anticipation loop — 30 times per second (every 33 ms) Planned

      Camera frame → vision encoder → foothold prediction → feature lookup → future body-sensing predictor, on the GPU within 15 ms. The control loop always uses the newest prediction available. Because the loops are not synchronised, a prediction can be 0 to about 80–100 ms old when used. The policy is told how much of each prediction's look-ahead is left (rem_60, rem_200, rem_400), and predictions older than about 150 ms are switched off.

      What the policy sees: 1,125 numbers every 20 ms

      The policy's input was widened once, at the blind-trunk stage, to hold every arm's blocks. Blocks an arm is not allowed to use are held at exactly zero, so the extra input connections stay at zero and change nothing. Hover a segment for details (widths are to scale).

      What the critic sees: the privileged information contract (683 numbers)

      Reinforcement learning uses two networks. The policy (also called the actor) decides the joint targets and must run on the robot, so it may only use what real sensors provide. The critic only judges how good a situation is, to make training efficient; it exists only in the simulator, so it is allowed to "cheat" with true values the robot can never measure. Every arm gets exactly the same cheat sheet so that no arm gains from a better critic. It is computed before the action is chosen and does not depend on it.

      Training signals (what each part learns from)

      Rules about which signal may change which part

      • The reinforcement-learning signal never changes the encoders or estimators; they learn only from their own prediction and estimation tasks. tested
      • The critic's learning never changes the policy, and the helper tasks never change the policy or the critic. tested
      • The physics quiz and the future-prediction loss may not reach the foothold prediction module through the foothold location (it is detached before the feature lookup). planned test
      • After widening, the policy must reproduce the previous policy's output to within 0.000001 on 10,000 random inputs (measured: exactly 0.0). tested

      The rhythm clock (central pattern generator) — training scaffolding

      Walking is bootstrapped with a rhythm clock CPG: each leg gets a sine/cosine phase (12 numbers). Legs move in two alternating groups of three (a "tripod gait": right-front, right-back and left-middle together, then the other three), each leg on the ground half the time. The clock runs at forward command ÷ 0.20 m per second, never slower than 0.8 steps per second (below that the robot used to freeze) and never faster than 2.5; each episode starts at a random phase. Several rewards pay for following the clock (lift the toe on a half-sine during swing, sweep the hip backwards during stance, carry about one third of the body weight per planted foot).

      When the robot is told to stand still, the clock stops and its 12 inputs are set to zero (the policy had learned to keep "swinging" legs lifted when it saw a frozen clock).

      Planned removal in the depth stage (S2): the clock inputs and every clock-based reward are removed together over the first 30 % of the stage (the clock is dropped for whole episodes, never step by step). Walking must then be held up by rewards that do not use the clock: the swing-height floor (toe must lift at least 4 cm) and the planted-foot stillness penalty, both already active.

      Training plan

      Six training stages, each with pass/fail criteria

      Training is split into stages that add one difficulty at a time. Every comparison arm goes through the same stages with the same budgets (fixed in simulator steps before the main runs), so differences between arms come from the method, not the recipe. Budgets are fixed by a pilot run and then never used to stop an individual run early.

      Batch size note

      The specification asks for 8,192 simulations × 16 steps per update in stages S0–S1. This project uses 4,096 × 16 (the size the original policy was trained with, and the compute limit; deviation D1). The camera stages will run with N_v simulations × T_v steps per update, at least 24,576 steps, for every arm including the blind ones, so no arm gets a different batch.

      Comparison arms

      Seven versions of the robot's brain, compared under one recipe

      An "arm" is one version of the system trained and tested the same way as the others. Each arm differs from its neighbour by a small, named set of changes, so each comparison answers exactly one question. The decisive pair is F (the proposed method) vs B2: they differ only by the future prediction and its training signals. None of the arms beyond the shared blind trunk is built yet.

      How results will be judged

      • Paired seeds: every arm (except B1-K) branches from the same blind-trunk seeds, so each F seed has a matching B2 seed.
      • Seeds: B2 and F aim for 8 seeds each (never fewer than 5).
      • Main test: a paired t-test on the per-seed difference F − B2, with a 95 % confidence interval.
      • Also reported: a sign test, the interquartile mean with bootstrap confidence intervals, the probability that F beats B2, and every per-seed difference.
      • Evaluation size: at least 200 episodes per test condition per seed, with deterministic actions and identical random conditions across arms.
      • What is measured: the main behaviour measures are stride shortening and longer foot contact before slippery ground. The headline measures are how early the robot adapts (lead time) and the peak ground force.
      • Hardware: the median seed of B0, B2 and F is tested on the real robot; B1-M is recommended too.
      • If compute runs short, cut in this order: B3, then B1-K to 3 seeds, then B1-M to 3 seeds. The main camera stage is never shortened.

      Rewards

      The final blind-trunk reward (23 terms + a fall penalty)

      The policy learns by maximising the sum of these terms, added up every 20 ms (each weight is multiplied by 0.02 s). Every change is checked with a "reward economy" calculation before training: walking at the command must earn clearly more than standing still, which must earn more than falling. Policies reliably find the cheapest behaviour the reward allows, so each term below exists because a specific unwanted behaviour appeared without it.

      Changes on the way to the camera stages

      • All terms marked clock leave together with the rhythm clock over the first 30 % of the depth stage (S2). The swing-height floor and the planted-foot stillness penalty stay.
      • Known imperfection D30: the rear hip servos are asked to go past their ±55° limit slightly more often than in the standing-and-walking policy (0.026–0.029 vs 0.015 radians per step). It costs about 0.05 of roughly 40 reward per second and the servo simply clips it; to revisit when designing the S2 reward.
      • The "body part near ground" term (weight −30) is still active in the blind trunk: an older schedule was supposed to fade it out, but that schedule is switched off from S0a onward. Worth confirming it is intended.
      Progress

      From the first walking policy to the blind trunk

      Phase 1 produced a walking policy. Phase 2 rebuilt it into a policy that could run on the real robot (S0) and then into the shared blind trunk (S1). Each step below lists what was tried, what went wrong, why, and what fixed it. Every number is from a recorded evaluation (usually 256 or 1,024 episodes, deterministic actions).

      Phase 1 — first walking policy (flat ground, simulated perfect motors)

      A reinforcement-learning environment with a rhythm clock produced a rough tripod gait on flat ground: pure body sensing, no history, no randomisation, ideal motors (stiff 3 N·m joints, twice the real servo's stall torque) and the true body speed as an input. It is the policy the paper calls π_base.

      A 256-episode re-evaluation in Phase 2 found: forward speed 0.159 m/s (the earlier 0.209 m/s came from one 10-second run), 0 % falls, feet on the ground 65 % of the time, and two "crutch" legs: the right-middle and right-rear legs lifted only 0.10–0.11 times per second against about 0.8 for the others. Its reward was also dominated by a hip term that paid for buzzing back and forth rather than for pushing the body forward.

      Phase 2.0 — fixes and the first realistic policy (S0a), 2026-09-21

      Correctness fixes made before any new training: the toe position is computed from the leg geometry everywhere (it used a fixed height that is only right at rest); the rhythm clock floor went from 0.2 to 0.8 steps per second with a random start phase; the contradictory hip rewards were replaced by one signed term; the gait's "half the time on the ground" setting was given a single source; the per-foot force target was corrected from 4 N to one third of the real weight (the robot weighs 3.22 kg, not 1.2 kg); friction between foot and ground now multiplies (the simulator's default averages them); a 0.15 kg camera mast was added to every arm.

      The control arm that changed the plan

      The specification requires one run from a random start with the same setup, to check whether continuing from the old policy is worth it. If the random start reaches the pass criteria within 1.2 × the effort of the continued lineage (26,596 iterations), the continuation is dropped for every arm. It reached them at 8,000 iterations (0.30 ×), and it turned symmetrically where the continued lineage never could, which showed the lopsidedness was inherited from the Phase 1 policy.

      Gait corrections that the pass/fail tests did not catch

      The first from-scratch policy passed every test, yet videos showed the middle and rear feet tucked almost under the hips, toes swinging in and out in the air, and legs passing through each other (the simulator does not collide a leg with its neighbour). New probes measured each problem, and the standing-and-walking policy was retrained from scratch with three new reward terms.

      Phase 2.2 — the blind trunk (S1), 2026-09-24 → today

      Built: ground materials that vary by location; the body-sensing encoder; the velocity and foot-force estimator; the physics quiz; the 1,125-number policy input and the 683-number critic contract; a training loop that also trains the helpers; and the tests that guard which signal may change which part. Collecting experience first took 5.9 s per iteration; after making the material patches visual-only (the physics uses one ground mesh) and running the encoder at half precision it takes 4.0 s.

      Three pilot runs (seed 42, 50-step memory window)

      • Pilot 1: passed every test, but legs crept together (gap down to −0.6 cm) and the toe lift sank to 3.5–4.0 cm. Stopped at 8,650 to diagnose.
      • Pilot 2: leg-proximity penalty ×4 (−1,600) and swing-height floor raised to 4 cm. Clearance held (+0.8 to +1.3 cm). The reward gain dropped below 1.5 % at 8,000, which fixed the blind-trunk budget at 8,000 iterations. Front legs still drifted inward.
      • Pilot 3 (accepted): toe-lift target 4 cm and a separate 10 cm landing target for the front legs. A pre-declared watch rule stopped it at 4,500 (rear hips hitting their limit, lift overshooting); resumed after review and finished at 8,000.
